**In 1996 Jensen Huang flew to Tokyo to tell his most important customer that the chip his company had built for it was wrong. Nvidia had bet its first architecture on a rendering method the rest of the industry was abandoning, the console deal it depended on had collapsed, and the company had roughly thirty days of cash. He asked Sega to keep funding him anyway. Sega turned the money it owed into a [$5 million investment](https://thenextweb.com/news/nvidia-sega-1995-5-million-rescue), and in August 1997 Nvidia shipped the RIVA 128, built for the industry standards it had bet against, with about a month of payroll left. Thirty years later, on 11 November 2026, Nvidia's board accepted his resignation as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately. Between those two dates sits most of what I know about how to build: a man who could shed his company's skin in a month, and a company that had grown into the shape of one man.**

*Architecting Alpha, Part XIII*

[Nobody Is Actually Building](/posts/nobody-is-actually-building/) was the diagnosis. [Everything Converts](/posts/everything-converts/) argued that when everything reproducible becomes commensurable, the premium does not disappear; it relocates to whatever stays non-reproducible. [The Rationing Regime](/posts/the-rationing-regime/) argued that in the physical stack the rent goes to whoever controls allocation. This essay is the human layer underneath all three: who captures the relocated premium, and how that person is built.

It is also the lens I use for every decision that matters to me: what to build, what to back, which assets to buy, and which founders to sit across from. The same architecture runs through venture capital, private equity, infrastructure and energy. I have stopped believing they are different disciplines. They are one discipline applied at different points on the curve.

## The claim, and how it fails

**When intelligence is cheap, the scarce asset is the person who can run one loop and keep running it: shed the identity the old world rewarded, slay the fat dragons sitting on what the old world hoarded, and build what is not yet in the data. Venture capital is that loop pointed at knowledge that does not exist yet. Private equity is the same loop pointed at knowledge that exists but has not been applied. The people who can run it are dangerous in a precise sense. They can change the state of the world against resistance, faster than the resistance can organise, and they can correct themselves when they are wrong. My conjecture is that the gap between those people and everyone else widens, not narrows, as the models get better.**

Five ways this is wrong. It may be survivorship bias dressed up as a method. It may be a theory of founders that the steward era has already refuted. The founder it leans on most resigned last week, and the concentration that made him great may be the lesson rather than the exception. "Dangerous" cuts both ways, and the most dangerous person in a company is often the one who built it. And the premise that models cannot build outside their training set was weakened last month, when two labs produced the same new mathematical result on the same day. I take all five at the end.

## Part I. The future is not in the data

Popper's argument against predicting history is short enough to carry in your head. The course of human affairs depends on the growth of knowledge. We cannot predict what we will know in future, because if we could, we would know it now. So we cannot predict the course of anything that depends on knowledge not yet created. Deutsch sharpened the point: a forecast of what people will discover is not a prediction, it is a prophecy. Alan Kay turned the whole argument into an instruction in 1971. The best way to predict the future is to invent it.

That instruction is the entire job description of a builder, and in the last three years it has become literal. Every large model is trained on what has already been written. It interpolates beautifully inside that distribution and gets cheaper to run every quarter. That is the new world in one sentence: everything inside the training set is being priced towards the cost of electricity.

People have training sets too. Parents, schools, an industry's norms, a peer group, a feed. Groupthink is a human training set, and most careers and most companies are interpolations inside one: copy what worked, paste it into a slightly different context, optimise. That used to be a respectable living. It is now the exact work a model does for the price of a token, and it is being repriced accordingly.

So the useful question for a founder, an operator or an investor is not "what will the future look like?" It is "what can I make exist that is not yet in anyone's data, and what has to be true of me to make it?" The three verbs in the title are my answer to the second half.

## Part II. Shed skin

A snake does not shed because it is old. It sheds because it is growing. Its skin does not stretch, so growth means leaving the old one behind, and the young snakes that grow fastest shed most often. The days before a shed have a name among keepers: the snake is "in blue". Fluid gathers under the old skin, the scale over each eye turns milky, and the animal is half blind and more defensive than usual. When a shed fails, the old skin that stays on can tighten as the body grows and cut off the circulation to whatever it surrounds. Retained caps over the eyes leave the animal unable to see properly.

I have not found a better description of what it feels like to update. The period before you let go of an identity is the period when you see worst and defend hardest. And the skin you refuse to shed does not simply stay with you. It constricts you as you grow, and the first thing it takes is your vision.

Every version of you is a conjecture about who you need to be for the next problem. The operator who was right for the last stage, the story you told investors last year, the market you understood, the network that got you here: all of them were good conjectures, and all of them will be refuted by growth if you are doing anything worth doing. Knowledge grows by conjecture and criticism. Identity does too. Mental sovereignty, the thing I care most about, is the capacity to run that loop on yourself rather than let the consensus run it for you.

Huang in Tokyo is the cleanest case I know. NV1 was not a small mistake. It was the company's founding architecture, and the market was moving to a standard that made it obsolete. He did not defend it. He killed it, told the customer it was dead, and asked to be funded to build its replacement on the standard he had bet against. The motto that came out of that period, ["our company is thirty days from going out of business"](https://spectrum.ieee.org/view-from-the-valley/at-work/start-ups/nvidia-cofounders-remember-their-startup-roller-coaster-ride), outlived the cash crisis by decades.

Notice what he did not shed. He never abandoned the conviction that computing would be accelerated, that graphics was the wedge, and that whoever owned the platform would own the era. He was immovable about the problem and ruthless about the solution. That combination is the hardest-to-vary idea in this essay: **conviction about the destination, falsifiability about the route.** Most people who fail have it the other way round. They are flexible about where they are going and stubborn about how they get there.

## Part III. Slay fat dragons

In the Völsunga saga, Fáfnir is not born a dragon. He kills his father for a hoard of cursed gold, carries it off to a heath, and lies on it until he becomes one. For a long time nobody fights him, because nobody needs to. He does nothing with the gold. He guards it.

That is the most precise description of a private equity target I know. A fat dragon is not a weak company. It is often a strong one: a big balance sheet, real customers, a brand people know, assets that took decades to accumulate. What makes it a dragon is that it stopped shedding skin. It is still lying on the hoard in the shape the old world rewarded, and the gap between what the hoard is worth in its current owner's hands and what it would be worth in better ones is the opportunity.

The textbook definition of private equity is accurate but bloodless: identify an existing opportunity, buy an underpriced asset at a discount, use a structure of debt and equity to take control of it, improve it, and then either hold it or sell it. Here is the version I actually work from. **A discount is the market's price for an owner who stopped evolving.** Private equity is the business of buying that discount, forcing the shed the owner would not do, and keeping the difference.

Sigurd's method matters too. He does not meet Fáfnir head on. He digs a pit in the path the dragon takes to the water and strikes upward as it crawls over him, because the armour is on top. The armour of an incumbent is its brand, its market position and its relationships, and you do not win by attacking those. The soft underside is the operating model and the balance sheet: how the work actually gets done, how much of the cost base is habit, how the capital is arranged. That is where value creation in a buyout lives, and it is where AI now cuts deepest. Every incumbent still running a pre-AI operating model is a dragon lying on gold it no longer knows how to spend. My conjecture is that the gap between those operating models and AI-native ones is the largest value-creation spread most of us will see in our careers.

Debt is the part people misread. Leverage is not only cheap money. It is a clock. It does to a portfolio company what thirty days of cash did to Nvidia in 1996: it removes the option of not shedding. That is why it works in the hands of an operator who can transform an asset quickly, and why it kills in the hands of one who cannot. In [The Restricted Group](/posts/the-restricted-group/) the failure was long-duration assets financed on short-duration terms. The general rule underneath it is that leverage magnifies whatever loop the owner is running. If the loop is shed, slay and build, leverage compounds it. If the loop is guard the hoard, leverage only shortens the time until the hoard changes hands.

And the saga carries a warning every buyer should read twice. The gold is cursed. Whoever holds it is pulled towards becoming the next dragon. Funds that bought companies to transform them and ended up merely holding them, engineering the hoard instead of putting it to work, became the thing they were built to slay. The cure is the same as it is for the founder: keep shedding.

The dragons are not only companies. These are the ones I meet every week, by name.

**The incumbent** sits on a market. It is the most obvious dragon and the least dangerous, because it is visible.

**The consensus** sits on permission. It decides what counts as a sensible career, a fundable company, a reasonable valuation, an acceptable risk. It is the training set with teeth. Slaying it does not mean being contrarian for the sake of it; a contrarian is still interpolating, just against the consensus instead of with it. It means holding a conjecture the consensus does not contain and testing it against reality instead of against opinion.

**The long middle** sits on time. It is the stretch after the excitement of starting and before the market pays you, when the work is unrewarded, the people around you begin to doubt, and your own energy drains. Nvidia's share price went roughly nowhere for most of the decade after it launched CUDA. That is what conviction looks like from the outside: years in which you are not paid for being right.

**Contempt** sits on your reputation. Rejection, ridicule, being underestimated. The only stance I have found that works is to treat it as tuition rather than a verdict: to absorb a great deal of other people's contempt without losing the plot, and without shrinking the vision to fit their opinion of you.

**Your former self** is the fattest dragon of all, because it sits on everything you have already won. Fáfnir was not born a dragon. Neither is anyone else.

When Sigurd roasts the dragon's heart, he burns his thumb, puts it in his mouth, and suddenly understands the speech of birds. What the birds tell him is that the man who advised him to kill the dragon now plans to kill him. I take that seriously as a description of what slaying actually yields. You do not just get the gold. You get knowledge you could not have acquired any other way, including knowledge about the people around you. Operating knowledge, earned inside the asset, is the one thing a model cannot hand you.

## Part IV. Build

If private equity buys knowledge that exists but has not been applied, venture capital backs knowledge that does not exist yet. Venture capital is the discipline of finding unique opportunities early and backing them before they are legible to anyone else. Venture finds the egg. Private equity slays the dragon that sat on the nest too long.

This is where Popper stops being a garnish and becomes the method. If the thing you are backing depends on knowledge not yet created, no model can tell you whether it will work, because the answer is not in any data that exists. So you are not underwriting a forecast. You are underwriting two things: a conjecture about a problem, and a person who can run the error-correction loop faster than the market can kill them.

CUDA is the canonical example. In 2006 Nvidia turned its graphics chip into a general parallel computer and then spent years funding a platform with almost no market, paid for by its gaming business. In 2012 a neural network called AlexNet, trained on two Nvidia GPUs, won the ImageNet competition by a wide margin, and the market arrived at a platform that was already built. Nobody in 2006 knew deep learning would happen. Nvidia built the thing that would be ready if it did, and survived long enough to find out. That is what building outside the training set looks like in practice: not a prophecy, but a bet that is cheap to keep alive and enormous if it lands.

The new world adds one more layer, and it is where I now spend most of my time. Models can generate text, code, images and, increasingly, mathematics. They cannot generate an electron, a permit, a grid connection, a tonne of steel, or the trust of a community that has to live next to a data centre. Infrastructure and energy are outside the training set by nature. In [The Rationing Regime](/posts/the-rationing-regime/) I argued that compute is now allocated rather than bought; allocation matters because the binding inputs are physical, political and slow. The builder who can work across bits, atoms, electrons and capital, and who can design how the whole system fits together before the deal is signed, is building in the one place a model cannot follow.

So here is how I decide what to build and what to back, compressed to a rule. **Problems tell me what. Trends tell me when. Constraints tell me where the rent is.** I start from a problem in the world that is real and expensive, never from a trend, because a trend is the training set's view of the future. I use trends only to time the entry. Then I look for the binding constraint in the stack, the scarce input everyone else will need, because that is where value concentrates when the trend arrives. Everything else is a pitch deck.

Building also means seeing it through, which is a different skill from starting. It means holding the end state in view from the first step to the last, and being able to explain that end state to an engineer, an investor, a customer and a regulator, each in their own language, on the days when the evidence is thin. Most visions do not die because they were wrong. They die in the long middle because the person holding them put them down.

## Part V. The dragon inside the founder

Now the hard part.

On 11 November, after thirty-three years, Nvidia's board accepted Huang's resignation as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately, according to the company's [Form 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001045810/000104581026000082/nvda-20261111.htm). Colette Kress, the chief financial officer, became interim chief executive while keeping the finance role, and Harvey C. Jones became the company's first-ever board chair. The resignation followed reports about Huang's personal relationships and an independent board investigation opened days earlier. Nothing has been established, and I will not speculate about what has not.

I have said for a long time that you cannot take Jensen out of Nvidia without changing what Nvidia is. That conjecture is now being tested in public, and I am not going to predict the result, because the result depends on knowledge nobody has yet: who the board chooses, what that person sees, what the next architecture is. What I can state is the structural lesson, and it does not depend on the details.

The concentration of vision that builds a category also concentrates the risk. A company engineered around one person's judgement, with dozens of direct reports and [no anointed successor](https://thenextweb.com/news/jensen-huang-resigns-as-nvidia-ceo-after-33-years-effective-immediately), can turn one person's crisis into the company's crisis inside a week. In the end it was not a market or a competitor that forced the change. It was governance: the board, its duties, and the codes and covenants that bind directors whether or not anyone is in the mood.

Uber is the earlier version of the same lesson. Travis Kalanick built a category with an aggression few founders could match, and resigned as chief executive in June 2017 under pressure from investors over culture and governance. Under Dara Khosrowshahi the company became durable, and for 2023 it reported its first full year of operating profit. The traits that create a category can become the liabilities that threaten it.

This is why the definition of dangerous in this essay has a third clause, and why the clause is not decoration. A builder who cannot correct himself is not dangerous to the incumbents. He is dangerous to his own company. Self-governance is part of the builder's architecture: mental sovereignty turned inward, the willingness to put structures around your own failure modes before the world does it for you. The most dangerous dragon in any founder-led company is the one wearing the founder's face. Slaying it does not mean leaving. It means building the board, the succession and the bench while you are still the reason the company works.

## Part VI. Founders and stewards

Apple is usually told as a founder story, and half of it is one. Steve Jobs was pushed out in 1985, the company drifted through the following decade, and by 1997 it was close to insolvency. Apple's acquisition of NeXT, announced in December 1996, brought him back, and what followed was the iMac, the iPod and the iPhone.

The other half is the one founder-worshippers skip. Under Tim Cook, Apple shipped new categories, the Watch and AirPods, and carried out one of the largest platform transitions in its history, moving the Mac to its own silicon from 2020, while multiplying the company's value many times over. "Apple stopped innovating after Jobs" is true of 1985 to 1997 and false of the Cook era.

The useful distinction is not founder good, steward bad. Stewards optimise the current S-curve, and they can compound enormous value doing it. Founders jump to the next curve. The question for an investor, and for a board, is which one the company needs at its current stage, and whether the person in the seat can tell the difference.

That maps cleanly onto the two disciplines. Venture capital backs curve-jumpers. Private equity often installs stewards, and in a mature, stable asset that is the correct call. But the best private equity in the new world will have to do something harder: install the curve-jump inside a fat incumbent. That takes an operator who can think like a founder while respecting the hoard enough not to burn it.

Elon Musk is my favourite test of all this. My view, and it is a view, is that he is not necessarily the smartest person alive but he is the best entrepreneur: he architects the vision, understands it end to end, builds the systems and teams that execute it, recruits the engineers, raises the capital, and sees it through. The first three Falcon 1 launches failed. The fourth reached orbit in September 2008, the same year Tesla and SpaceX both came close to running out of money. The same case carries the same shadow as Huang's. The governance controversies and the concentration of key-person risk are real, and an investor who prices the vision without pricing the concentration is not underwriting. He is admiring.

## Part VII. The lens

Put the pieces together and they become a diligence lens. I use it on founders when I look at venture-shaped bets, on management teams when I look at assets, and on myself. It is a working conjecture, not a proven model, and I expect to revise it.

1. **Problem conviction.** Can they state the problem in one sentence that survives an hour of attack?
2. **Solution falsifiability.** Have they killed their own wrong idea at least once, and can they talk about it without flinching?
3. **Endurance record.** What did they do in their worst six months so far?
4. **Contempt and empathy.** How do they talk about the people who rejected them? A builder who absorbs contempt without becoming contemptuous can lead people through failure. One who cannot will lose the team the first time things go wrong.
5. **Magnetism.** Who has followed them with time or money before there was proof?
6. **Cash discipline.** Do they know their runway in days?
7. **Self-governance.** What structures protect the company from their own failure modes?
8. **Stage fit.** Does this company need a founder or a steward right now, and do they know which one they are?

Empathy deserves one more word, because it is the trait most often mistaken for softness. Used well, empathy is leverage: knowing what each person in the system needs to hear and feel, in their own language, so that the engineer, the investor, the customer and the regulator each see the same vision from their own side of the table. A vision has to be told before it is believed. The builder who can explain it in only one language builds alone.

## Part VIII. My own skin

I grew up in a small railway town that had become a truckers' town, the kind of place where the long-haul rigs sleep before the next leg. One high school, one primary school, an ordinary middle-class family, and nobody around me who could teach me about business or money. My first year out of school I studied strategic management and played semi-professional rugby in an academy, which is where most of my work ethic comes from. In my second year I dropped out and sold the car I was driving. There was no plan B.

At school I had moved between friend groups of every kind and ended up as the one who made peace between them and brokered the deals. It took me years to recognise that as my first training in negotiation. What followed was a long self-education in why people do what they do: human behaviour and evolutionary biology, Kahneman's two systems, crowd psychology, persuasion, neuromarketing through Copenhagen Business School, negotiation, game theory, complexity economics, and eventually algorithms and the way narratives move whole populations. That path is what pulled me into artificial intelligence, which I am still studying at MIT.

For most of my career my skin was the fixer's. I would step into a company with my team, fix it with minimal capital, build the systems, slot in the operators and hire the chief executive, so that it could run without me. Venture building and systemisation, not a finance track, is what led me into venture capital and private equity. I learned more than one lesson the expensive way. The one I would put first is that committed capital is not capital. I once watched a round that investors had committed to simply fail to arrive, and the company had to keep going on what it actually had.

The last twelve months have been a sequence of sheds, and I can name them. The fixer who made other people's companies run without him became the builder of his own. The operator who improved portfolio companies became an operator-investor: I shared my thesis on content and AI with general partners, fund managers and bankers, and it led to new opportunities and responsibilities at the fund level. The software builder moved down the stack into atoms and electrons, where I now lead the architecture of infrastructure builds alongside the engineering teams, working out how the whole system fits together with our technology partners before the deal is made. Alongside that we are building companies inside our own ecosystem, and you will be able to learn more once we launch. Each of those sheds cost me something: a story I liked telling, a comfortable kind of competence, a few relationships that fitted the old shape and not the new one.

None of it felt heroic from the inside. Building is energising and draining, sometimes in the same hour. There were weeks of being in blue: half blind, more defensive than I like to admit, unsure whether the new skin would come. What I have learned is that the anxiety is not a sign you are on the wrong path. Usually it is the sign that the shed has started.

Three things drive me, in this order. The first is mental sovereignty: the ability to think for yourself, to resist groupthink, and to refuse to shrink your mind below what you are actually capable of. The second is poverty, which I consider the most important solvable problem we have. The third is helping people upgrade what I call their architecture of thinking, so that they can build: create value, add it to the economic system, and build for others as well as for themselves. Human building and world building are the same project at different scales.

## Part IX. Not utopia

I used to describe the destination as a kind of utopia. I have stopped, for a reason Popper gave in 1945. A blueprint for a perfect society cannot be criticised and corrected, so the people who try to impose one end up suppressing the criticism instead of fixing the blueprint. The alternative is not cynicism. It is Deutsch's optimism: problems are inevitable, and problems are soluble. There is no finished world at the end of this. There is an unbounded sequence of problems, each solved by a conjecture that will eventually be improved on, each solution creating the next and better problem.

That is a more demanding vision than utopia, not a smaller one. Utopia lets you stop once you arrive. The beginning of infinity never lets you stop, which is exactly why the capacity to keep shedding, keep slaying and keep building is the thing worth developing. Poverty will not be solved by a plan. It will be solved, if it is, by enough people learning to make things exist that did not exist before, and by institutions that let them keep doing it.

## How this could be wrong

It may be survivorship bias. For every founder who held a vision through a dark decade, many more held one and died, and nobody writes essays about them. Conviction is necessary but not sufficient. My answer is that the survivors were not conviction machines. They were criticism machines with a fixed destination, and that is visible in the record before the outcome was known: NV1 was killed in 1996, not in hindsight. But if you could show me a large sample of companies whose founders killed their own wrong architecture early and died at the same rate as those who did not, the method in this essay would be close to worthless.

The steward era may refute the founder thesis. Cook's Apple and Khosrowshahi's Uber compounded more value than their founders' versions did over comparable periods. If stewards are routinely better at the stage where most value is created, then "founder versus steward" is a story investors tell themselves, and the real skill is knowing when to replace the builder rather than how to back one.

Concentration may be the lesson, not the exception. If Nvidia without Huang loses its edge, my thesis that the builder's architecture matters is confirmed, and so is the risk that comes with it, and investors should price founder-led companies at a permanent discount for key-person risk. If Nvidia does well, the founder was less irreplaceable than I believed, and the platform, the team and the cadence carried more of the value than the man. Either way, it should change how I underwrite.

"Dangerous" may be the wrong virtue. The traits in this essay, the conviction, the tolerance for contempt, the appetite for leverage, are also the traits of founders who destroy companies and the people around them. Self-correction is the answer I offer. It may not be enough, because the person least able to see a founder's failure modes is usually the founder.

And the training set may not be the boundary I think it is. Last month two AI labs independently produced the same new result in complexity theory on the same day, which is hard to describe as interpolation, although neither result has yet been peer reviewed. If models can generate genuinely new knowledge, building outside the training set stops being a uniquely human advantage. My answer is that generating a result is not the same as choosing the problem, holding a bet through a decade, or moving atoms, electrons and people through the physical world. But that answer is itself a conjecture about what models cannot do, and conjectures of that kind have had a short half-life.

If one of those five breaks first, write to info@selfbuiltsystems.com and tell me which. Short and specific gets an answer.

Everyone reading this is lying on some hoard: a title, a market, a reputation, a way of working the old world paid for. The new world will not take it from you all at once. It will price it towards zero, quietly, until the day you notice the skin no longer fits and the dragon is wearing your face. So name three things before you close this page. The skin you need to shed. The dragon you need to slay. And the thing you will build that is not yet in anyone's data. Then see it through.
